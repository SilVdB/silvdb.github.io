We tend to measure intelligence by its outputs — the complexity of what a system knows, the breadth of what it can recall, the accuracy of its predictions. But this misses something more fundamental. <mark>Intelligence is not primarily about storage or retrieval. It is about selection.</mark> About which signals, out of the overwhelming noise of the world, become the ones that matter.

Attention is that selection mechanism. It is older than language, older than abstraction, older than the capacity to form concepts at all. And it turns out to be the architectural primitive that modern AI systems have, under sufficient optimization pressure, independently converged on.

## The biological layer

A human visual field spans roughly 200 degrees, but only 2 to 5 degrees of that field are processed at full resolution at any moment. The eye does not take in a scene — it samples it, rapidly repositioning to build a high-resolution composite from a sequence of narrow fixations called saccades. What drives those fixations is attention.

Attention in the brain operates as a hard bottleneck. The retina sends roughly 10 million bits per second to the visual cortex; conscious visual processing can handle perhaps 40 to 50 bits per second. <mark>Almost everything that enters the eye is discarded before it reaches awareness. What survives is what the system deemed worth attending to — which is itself a judgment, a prior, a theory of what will matter.</mark>

This constraint propagates up the cognitive stack. Working memory — the scratchpad of active reasoning — holds approximately four items at once. Every moment of thought is an act of selection under constraint. The mind does not reason with everything it knows. It reasons with whatever it has chosen to bring forward.

## The computational layer

The transformer architecture, introduced in 2017, solved a fundamental problem in sequence modelling: how do you let any token in an input attend to any other token, regardless of distance? Previous approaches — recurrent networks, convolutions — imposed structural constraints on how information could flow. Transformers removed them entirely.

The mechanism is called self-attention. For each token, the model learns three linear projections: a **query** (what am I looking for?), a **key** (what do I represent?), and a **value** (what information do I carry?). Each query is compared against every key in the sequence — the comparison is a dot product, scaled and passed through a softmax — and the result is a weight distribution over the values.

<mark>The architecture does not encode meaning directly. It encodes relevance relationships — a learned, dynamic map of which parts of the input should influence which other parts.</mark> What looks like understanding from the outside is the emergence of a sophisticated attention policy, trained across billions of examples to know what is worth noticing in any given context.

This is not a metaphor for intelligence. It is a direct implementation of the same principle the brain uses: allocate limited processing capacity according to a learned theory of relevance.

## What they share

The parallels are structural, not superficial. Both biological and transformer attention:

- Operate under explicit capacity constraints — working memory in one case, context window length in the other
- Use learned priors to bias which inputs receive processing
- Compute relevance dynamically, in relation to the current query or task
- Produce outputs that are weighted combinations of attended inputs, not simple selections

The convergence feels significant. It suggests that attention is not a design choice — it is a necessary structural feature of any intelligence that must act under finite resources. Any system that must select from a large input space in order to produce a coherent output will, under sufficient optimization pressure, develop something functionally equivalent to attention.

> What we call intelligence may simply be the quality of an attention policy — the sophistication of a system's theory of what is worth noticing.

The corollary is uncomfortable: a system with abundant compute and a poor attention policy will reason badly, regardless of how much it has been trained. Attention is not a feature of intelligence. It is its precondition. Every act of reasoning is preceded by an act of noticing. And every act of noticing is a small theory of what is real.
